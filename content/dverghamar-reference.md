Purpose

This is the authoritative working record for the Dwarven planet’s nonmagical physical setting, native biosphere, native dwarven genus, and the technological consequences of that setting. It consolidates the decisions made during the scientific review. The companion synthesis explains the causal history; the open-questions map tracks decisions that remain unresolved.

Canon-status rule

Explicitly selected qualitative outcomes are working canon. Numerical climate, ecological, engineering, and evolutionary values are design targets or scenario parameters unless a stated calculation actually derives them. Their placement beneath an older “Settled canon” heading does not turn them into demonstrated scientific results. Recommended values may be revised when coupled modeling exposes a conflict; open details remain unselected. Document 38 records this review’s corrections, calculations, and outstanding tests.

## Scope and continuity safeguards

Settled canon

The native dwarves evolved on this planet through ordinary evolutionary processes over deep time. They are not descendants of Erde humans, hobbits, or Thals.

The Thal catastrophe occurred on Erde. A surviving Neanderthal-descended population fled through the Otherworld and reached the Dwarven planet roughly five millennia before Erde’s present. That migration is a later demographic and cultural event. It did not create the planet, its climate, its geology, its biosphere, or the native dwarven genus.

The Otherworld crossing is an explicit narrative premise with no established scientific mechanism. It explains the Thals’ arrival in setting history, but supplies no continuing exemption from food, water, heat, reproduction, or planetary physics. The nonmagical physical and biological baseline is assessed independently of that crossing.

## Planetary name and linguistic usage

### Settled canon

Dverghamar is the canonical native name of the planet. It is the normal form in ordinary dwarven speech, narration, maps, diplomacy, and scientific discussion. The archaic or highly formal form is Dvergahamrar. It survives in ritual, foundational law, dynastic and civic inscriptions, solemn oaths, historical quotations, and deliberately elevated speech.

### Recommended semantic configuration

Dverg- is a fossilized ancestral element conventionally represented in Erde languages as "dwarven." It is no longer a productive standalone word for the people. Hamar carries a linked cluster of meanings: stone, crag, and hammer. Dverghamar therefore supports the translations "Dwarven Stone," "Stone of the Ancestral People," and, in craft-centered contexts, "Dwarven Hammer." The common singular presents the planet as one encompassing living stone.

Dvergahamrar preserves an older collective conception: "Crags of the Ancestral People" or "Hammers of the Ancestral People." Its plural form evokes the joined mountain roots, inhabited massifs, stone chambers, and regional peoples that together constitute the world. Dverghamar and Dvergahamrar are historically related lexical forms, not a productive modern singular-plural pair.

### Recommended usage and derivatives

Use Dverghamar on first reference and as the default name thereafter. Use Dvergahamrar only when the speaker or text has a reason to sound ancient, legal, sacred, or exceptionally formal. Hamrar, "the Crags," may occur as a poetic or intimate shortening, but is not a third official name. Dverghamaran is the recommended Erde-language adjective when one is needed; "of Dverghamar" and "native dwarven" are usually more natural. The selected general native ethnonym is Hamarkor, plural Hamarkorar, from hamar (crag or great rock mass) and kor (person). Hamarkorvar names the language family; Korvar remains the shared standard. Rakkor, plural Rakkorar, is retained for a regional people whose region is not yet selected. Menkor, plural Menkorar, remains available for another narrower regional, cultural or species identity; its exact assignment is open. Hamkor remains a proposed everyday contraction of Hamarkor, not a general sound change. Hamarvar retains its existing ridge-network language meaning.

Reader pronunciation is provisionally DVERG-ha-mar for Dverghamar and DVER-ga-HAM-rar for Dvergahamrar. These are accessibility pronunciations rather than a final reconstruction of native phonetics.

The forms are Norse- and Germanic-inspired authorial representations of an independent dwarven language. Their use does not establish that the native language descends from Old Norse or any Erde language.

## Stellar and orbital setting

### Settled canon

The selected planet is in a stable 1:1 spin–orbit state around an old, comparatively quiet early-M dwarf, with low obliquity and sufficiently small eccentricity to retain long-lived day and night hemispheres. Small libration and orbital weather cycles remain possible; “permanent” illumination is a climatic approximation near the moving geometric boundary, not perfectly fixed starlight at every point.

The system is old enough for slow oxygenation and extensive biological diversification. The biosphere evolved under synchronous illumination from near its beginning; the planet did not recently change from an Earthlike day-night cycle into its present state.

### Recommended working values

The bounded reference case uses a 0.50-solar-mass, 8.0-billion-year, near-solar-metallicity early-M star, approximated by 0.040 solar luminosities, 0.47 solar radii, and effective temperature about 3,765 K. At 1.20 Earth flux, the derived distance is 0.1826 AU and Kepler’s law gives a 40.30-Earth-day year and synchronous rotation period. These mutually consistent rounded inputs replace the former 0.16-AU, 1.56-flux illustration as the preferred screening case. They are conditional climate and escape inputs, not measured values, a unique stellar track, or immutable canon.

Residual stellar flaring must be low for an M dwarf but need not be zero. Atmospheric shielding, an old quiet star, and long biological adaptation are more credible than treating the star as completely inactive.

## Planetary bulk properties

### Settled canon

The selected rocky super-Earth has approximately 3 Earth masses and 1.4 Earth radii, giving surface gravity near 1.53 g and mean density about 6.0 g/cm³. A simple two-layer rocky-planet mass–radius comparison permits this combination with a core mass fraction near 0.14; this is one model fit, not a determined interior composition. Do not assume Earth’s exact iron fraction or add an unbudgeted thick hydrogen envelope. Core chemistry, mantle water, rheology and dynamo properties remain separate questions.

The present exchangeable surface-and-near-surface water inventory is approximately 0.15–0.20 Earth oceans: about 2.0–2.7 × 10^8 km³, or 200–270 m of global-equivalent water on this planet. This includes lakes, rivers, accessible groundwater, glaciers, and nightside ice, rather than a global ocean. Water chemically bound in the deep mantle is a separate, unresolved reservoir. The total alone does not establish whether lakes or an ocean form; basin topography and ice partition are also required.

### Consequences

At equal body shape and material strength, higher gravity raises supporting stresses and the work of vertical lifting. Compact dimensions, broad joints, good mechanical leverage, relatively short distal limbs, and shorter heart-to-brain distances can help, but are not inevitable adaptations. Near-surface air is also denser than Earth’s at comparable temperature, partly offsetting the extra weight in aerodynamic lift. Flight still has takeoff, power, and structural costs; high gravity alone does not ban it or predict humanoid dwarves.

Limited water makes extensive dry land and weak interregional ocean heat transport possible when basin topography and ice sequestration support that partition. It does not automatically guarantee either dryness or a large hemispheric temperature contrast. Sustained habitable provinces require water returned from cold traps through some combination of ice flow, marginal melting, sublimation and atmospheric transport, and connected groundwater.

## Atmosphere

### Settled canon

Reference lowland pressure is approximately 1.5 bar, with nitrogen dominant and roughly 14–15 percent oxygen by volume: a dry-air oxygen partial pressure of about 0.21–0.225 bar. This is a useful respiratory comparison with lowland Erde/Earth, not identical respiratory performance. Gas diffusivity, inspired water vapor, carbon dioxide, temperature, breathing mechanics, and altitude also matter. Atmospheric column mass p/g is about 10,000 kg/m², close to Earth’s; 1.5 bar here does not mean 1.5 times Earth’s atmospheric mass or heat capacity per unit area.

### Consequences

Reduced oxygen fraction can hinder ignition and sustained flames even with near-Earth oxygen partial pressure. The result depends on fuel, pressure, moisture, temperature, airflow, and gravity. Less frequent or less vigorous natural fire is a plausible working ecology, not an established universal smoldering regime. Dry prepared fuels, charcoal, insulated hearths, and controlled draft make cooking and metallurgy credible. Bellows increase delivery and mixing but do not raise oxygen percentage.

Fuel engines remain plausible if combustion chambers, fuels, compression, and air delivery are designed for the actual atmosphere. No single recipe of warmer intake air or higher compression guarantees success; heating intake air can reduce charge density and worsen heat rejection. Carbon monoxide, exhaust, particulates, and incomplete combustion require management. Hydropower remains the selected main industrial source on geographic and technological grounds, independently of whether a particular fuel burns readily.

### Recommended working values

The dry lowland reference composition for the next climate test is 84.4998 percent nitrogen, 14.5 percent oxygen, 0.9 percent argon-like inert gas, 0.10 percent carbon dioxide (1,000 ppm), and 2 ppm methane at 1.50 bar. Water vapor and aerosols remain prognostic or sensitivity variables. This yields 0.2175 bar dry oxygen, mean dry molar mass about 28.71 g/mol, a 5.39-km isothermal scale height at 280 K, and approximately 1.246 bar at 1,000 m. These are coherent starting inputs, not values shown to produce the selected climate. Carbon burial, oxidation, weathering, volcanism, and photochemistry must support any successful final composition.

## Surface climate and habitable corridor

### Settled canon

Central-dayside lowland surface temperatures of mostly 340–375 K (67–102°C), with dark dry basins locally approaching 385 K (112°C), remain the selected climate targets. They are not a solved global circulation result or a statement that every air layer, shaded surface, or aquifer has that temperature.

Deep-nightside surface temperatures of mostly 205–230 K (−68 to −43°C), with local cold traps at 190–205 K (−83 to −68°C), remain selected climate targets. Biologically active liquid refuges must have their own local temperature and water-activity budget; very cold bulk brine is not automatically habitable.

The intended natural surface ecology excludes established populations of large animals from the hot central dayside and deep nightside. Dayside heat and desiccation strongly constrain exposed life; on the nightside, darkness, negligible primary production, frozen water, and cold jointly prevent a broad large-animal food web. The stated cold alone cannot exclude every Earth-like endotherm. Supplied, insulated travelers or animals using exceptional warm refuges are distinct from a self-sustaining exposed biosphere. Microbes and protected subsurface organisms remain possible.

The approximate 4,500 km corridor, extending about 3,900 km dayward and 600 km nightward from the geometric terminator, is retained as a provisional mapping target. At the selected radius these are angular distances of about 25.1° and 3.85°. The actual viable boundary depends on temperature, humidity, accessible water, food, altitude, terrain, and organismal physiology, so neither width nor asymmetry has yet been scientifically derived. Local refuges and barren interruptions make this a set of provinces rather than a uniform habitable strip.

### Interpretation

Dayward provinces retain a local photon source but face heat and water limits. Nightward provinces become increasingly dependent on imported organic matter and local chemical energy. These mechanisms support an asymmetric ecological range, but they do not calculate the chosen distances. Terrain, clouds, winds, snow, and heat transport must establish where the hot and cold boundaries actually lie.

The corridor is thermally defined, not uniformly photosynthetic. Direct stellar illumination cannot plausibly extend through the entire 600 km nightward fringe. Peaks, atmospheric refraction, and scattered light can illuminate limited areas near the terminator, but most of the nightward margin is aphotic.

Several large surface ecological provinces occupy the viable belt. Narrow river valleys and mountain passes connect some provinces; mountain rain shadows opening into closed evaporative and salt basins form the principal long-lived barriers.

## Water cycle

### Settled canon

The largest surviving natural lakes cover roughly 50,000–150,000 square kilometres, with many smaller lakes and wetlands.

The selected persistent exposed evaporative terminal brine lakes occupy the outer dayside. Farther sunward, terminal basins are usually episodic salt pans. This distribution does not forbid ice-covered or subsurface brines elsewhere, including the nightside; salinity and liquid state alone do not establish biological habitability.

Outer-dayside lakes and wetlands provide local evaporation. A cool terminator does not automatically receive rain: precipitation requires actual moisture transport and uplift, and dry-land climate models can leave the terminator arid. The preferred refuge geography combines selected moist catchments with downhill glacier-outlet meltwater and connected aquifers. Every proposed river basin must close its precipitation, melt, evaporation, runoff, groundwater, and storage budget; nightside return is a mechanism to test rather than an assumed completed cycle.

Deep lakes commonly have oxygen-rich surface and coastal waters over permanently stratified, often anoxic deep layers. Not every Great-Lakes-scale body is permanently meromictic; the deepest, saltiest, and most sheltered lakes are the strongest candidates.

Mixing varies among lakes. Wind, inflows, salinity stratification, depth, ice, and topography determine whether deep water exchanges readily, mixes intermittently, or remains isolated. Decade-to-century partial mixing and millennial destructive overturn are retained only as provisional histories for selected deep stratified lakes, not universal recurrence laws. Exceptional inflows, landslides, seismic disturbance, changing salinity, or volcanic conditions can destabilize some basins. Toxic gas release requires an accumulated gas reservoir; not every overturn produces a limnic eruption. Synchrony removes the ordinary solar day but does not by itself eliminate eccentricity-driven or other climatic cycles.

## Geological history

### Settled canon

The young planet differentiated into a metallic core, deep silicate mantle, and early crust after a magma-ocean phase.

Early hydrogen and steam evolution, atmospheric escape, magma chemistry, oxidation, impacts, mantle storage, and outgassing set the later secondary atmosphere and water inventory. An old quiet present-day M dwarf can have had a long luminous, active youth; survival of sufficient water is a necessary historical condition, not guaranteed by planetary mass. Abiotic oxygen can be consumed by reduced magma, crust, mantle material, and volcanic gases if their sink capacity is adequate. The adopted modern oxygen inventory is predominantly biological, sustained by a long-term balance of reduced-material burial and oxidative sinks rather than photosynthetic production alone.

Plate behavior is not determined by planetary mass. The preferred history begins with stagnant-lid or episodic-lid behavior and develops a predominantly mobile-lid regime through sustained weakening, recycling, and mantle stresses, with later reorganizations. This remains a selected conditional history; neither continuous subduction nor a fixed number of plates is derived from the chosen mass and age.

In the selected mobile-lid history, magmatism, subduction, collision, erosion, and sedimentation can produce basaltic plains, volcanic arcs, granite-like intrusions, metamorphic massif cores, tectonic lakes, and aquifers. Add soluble sedimentary belts, old lava tubes, fracture caves, and rock shelters beside productive waters for early natural cave habitats. Competent granite/gneiss is favored for later excavation but does not automatically contain extensive natural dissolution caves.

Active volcanic arcs are common, but native settlements favor old competent flanks, intrusive bodies, and stable basement rather than vents, young ash, or gas-prone caldera floors.

### Relief

Ordinary mountain relief is roughly 1–3 km above adjacent terrain. Major ranges commonly reach 3–4.5 km of local relief. Exceptional massifs can approach 5–6 km, but high gravity makes very tall, steep mountains harder to sustain than on Earth.

## Moons and other companion bodies

### Settled constraint

Any long-lived moons must satisfy Roche, Hill-region orbital-stability, and tidal-migration constraints. For a nearly circular star-synchronous planet, the prograde corotation radius is about 1.44 Hill radii, outside the stable satellite region; ordinary bound prograde moons therefore orbit faster than the planet spins and tend to migrate inward through tides raised on the planet. Small mass can slow migration, while excessively close placement accelerates it. Modest moons may produce tides and short-period cycles, but their survival and mutual forcing are conditional. No moons are required to supply most mantle heat or tectonic activity.

### Open

Moon count, masses, semimajor axes, resonances, eccentricities, and tidal properties remain open. Orbital N-body tests must be coupled to dissipative tidal evolution and uncertainties in the planet’s history; a short stable integration or placement inside the Hill sphere is insufficient for a 7–9-billion-year survival claim. A moon-poor or moonless system is the conservative comparison case. Distant companion planets may provide small secular forcing if their architecture remains stable and compatible with the selected low-eccentricity synchronous climate.

## Primary production and planetary food web

### Settled canon

Photosynthesis supplies almost all global net primary production. Chemosynthesis can provide all local primary production in an isolated cave or geothermal oasis but remains a small fraction of the planetary total.

The conservative producer baseline is oxygenic photosynthesis using visible and some far-red light through two coupled photochemical stages, analogous in principle to known terrestrial systems. Extended far-red use has empirical precedent, but does not establish efficient growth under every M-dwarf spectrum or arbitrary infrared wavelengths. Extra serial photochemical stages remain optional speculation. Pigments, reflectance, heat rejection, and atmospheric filtering can produce varied vegetation colors; an early-M star does not require black or purple plants.

Major producer forms include microbial mats, low groundcovers, reed- or frond-like stands, squat shrubs, woody or columnar forms, phytoplankton, and periphyton. Exact body plans remain open.

Productivity may be limited by water, usable photons, nitrogen, phosphorus, trace nutrients, temperature, or combinations that vary by province. Uplift, weathering, fresh ash, glacial grinding, and hydrothermal exchange can resupply mineral nutrients; biological nitrogen fixation requires its own energy and micronutrients. Nutrient recycling is incomplete, so burial, erosion, leaching, and replacement fluxes must be included in persistent ecosystems and agriculture.

Detrital pathways are unusually important. Fungi, microbes, burrowers, lake-bottom communities, carrion feeders, and cave consumers recycle photosynthetically fixed matter as it moves away from the illuminated wet belt.

### Surface distribution

The dayward photic wet belt supplies nearly all biological energy. Near the terminator, usable light depends strongly on sun-facing slopes, elevation, cloud structure, and scattered light.

Most active life over the hostile hemispheres is microbial. Dayside surface microbes are usually inconspicuous, with occasional visible blooms or crusts when local moisture permits. Airborne microbes are mostly dormant travelers, with brief activity in rare liquid cloud droplets.

On the deep nightside, active near-surface microbes are rare and confined mainly to brine veins and geothermal fractures. Naturally occurring macroscopic life reaches only into cool aquifers at the sunward margin; the deep dayside contains microbes only.

## Nightside geothermal oases

### Settled canon

The selected ordinary isolated-oasis ecology has sparse finger-length or smaller consumers, with many poorer sites supporting microbes alone. This is a conservative ecological design, not a universal physiological size ceiling. Larger animals require a separately demonstrated production, oxygen, maintenance, and reproduction budget.

Microbes gain usable free energy where suitable reductants and electron acceptors meet—for example geological hydrogen or reduced sulfur with oxidants supplied through ice or groundwater. Geological heat helps maintain liquid habitat but is not itself animal food. Donor and acceptor fluxes jointly limit production. Sparse consumers can stay active only where ongoing local production covers maintenance, growth, and reproduction; some poorer oases contain microbes alone. Favorable melt pulses can temporarily increase resources, but floods may also dilute substrates, scour habitat, or deplete oxygen.

Gradual storage followed by threshold drainage or ice-dam failure can connect some neighboring oases irregularly. Regional century-to-millennial intervals remain examples, not calculated recurrence laws. Thin cold ice can stay frozen to its bed: thickness, geothermal flux, ice advection, pressure, storage, and hydraulic gradients must first permit a liquid reservoir and an outlet. Successive animal generations persist between floods only where local production suffices; floods can also destroy habitat.

## Lakes and aquatic consumers

### Settled canon

Common large native lake animals reach roughly 2–5 m and hundreds of kilograms. Rare forms can approach 1–3 tonnes.

Such sizes are most credible in productive oxygenated surface and coastal waters. Permanently anoxic deep layers restrict large active animals and favor microbes, small anaerobic or low-oxygen specialists, detrital fall, and episodic recolonization after mixing.

## Natural caves and subsurface life

### Settled canon

Natural macroscopic cave communities are limited by the rate and quality of energy delivery. Roots, floods, animal inputs, surface detritus, light shafts, or strong redox interfaces can support larger communities along connected paths. Depth and distance alone do not define the boundary; a shallow sealed cave can be poorer than a deeper well-supplied conduit. No broad natural kilometre-deep dwarf food web is inferred.

Volcanic and hydrothermal caves can support sulfur-, hydrogen-, iron-, or methane-based food webs where reductants meet oxidants. They cannot support large natural dwarf populations at kilometre depths without imported or deliberately cultivated photosynthetic energy.

Engineered dwarven cities at one or two kilometres depth are therefore not natural closed ecosystems. They depend on food, oxygen management, water circulation, waste recycling, and energy gathered from the broader inhabited belt.

## Dwarven agriculture

### Settled canon

Potato-like geophytes are plausible in shallow sunward chambers, terraces, and light wells: illuminated shoots capture photons while protected storage tissues develop below. Yield is limited by the light-collecting area and usable photon flux, not underground floor area. Mirrors and glazing incur losses, low stellar elevation causes self-shading, and continuous light requires native physiological adaptation rather than assuming Earth crops tolerate it unchanged.

Fungal crops convert plant residues and other suitable organic substrates into edible biomass; they do not create net energy. Feedstock carbon is divided among edible yield, respiration, waste, and residual substrate, so crop and fungal calories must not be counted twice. Decomposers recycle nutrients but cannot replace nutrients permanently exported or buried. Dung and food-waste streams need processing compatible with crop health and pathogen control.

Mirrors, light shafts, selective glazing, shades, and cool-air circulation can extend shallow agriculture. Electric grow lights are too energetically expensive to serve as the normal source of staple calories at the established technological level.

## Dwarven settlement envelope

### Settled canon

Dwarves strongly prefer underground settlement. Where underground and surface options provide equal value, they choose underground. Surface facilities exist only where exposure provides a clear advantage that cannot reasonably be reproduced below ground.

Major settlements retain a preferred depth range of about 200–800 m, with some deeper districts and rare strongholds around 1.5–2 km; 2.2–2.3 km remains an exceptional engineering target. These are conditional siting envelopes, not depths made habitable by dwarf robustness. At 2 km, a simple 2,700 kg/m³ rock column implies about 81 MPa vertical overburden stress before local tectonic stresses and excavation effects. Feasibility depends on competent rock, manageable groundwater, low regional temperatures and heat flow, sufficiently small supported spans, and reliable heat removal.

Mines can reach approximately 2.5 km, while rare expeditions may descend to roughly 3 km. These are not equivalent to permanent residential depth because heat rejection, ventilation, water control, evacuation, and supply costs rise sharply.

Underground rail normally runs 30–200 m beneath stable surface, with deeper local sections where topography, hazards, or fortification justify them.

Small, heavily supplied nightside strongholds may exist roughly 3,000–4,000 km from the terminator. They are engineered outposts, not self-sufficient natural ecosystems.

### Mountain settlements

Mountain cities can stand 1–2 km above neighboring valleys while remaining inside granite- or gneiss-rich massifs. Altitude relative to the pressure datum and vertical or lateral rock cover are separate quantities: a chamber high in a mountain is not thermally equivalent to one the same distance below a lowland. Rare high citadels around 2.5–3 km elevation require adapted occupants, controlled activity, or selective pressurization. Pressurization is an engineering option, not a universal requirement for naturally altitude-adapted natives.

Surface observatories, intakes, riverworks, ports, fields, roads, quarries, and defensive works remain minimal and function-specific.

## Dwarven technology and industrial power

### Settled canon

Hydropower from selected, reliably supplied twilight-region catchments is the principal industrial source. It requires usable head and low-flow discharge after downstream and ecological allocations, not simply mountains or a large annual rain estimate. Pumped storage consumes net energy. Surface fuel engines provide mobile/site-specific power; limited geothermal plants require suitable geology and heat sinks.

Electricity is present but limited. It serves rail systems, communications, pumps, selected lighting, controls, workshops, and emergencies. The civilization does not require digital computing.

Mechanical engines can reach the necessary industrial threshold without converting all output to electricity. Direct shaft power, compressed air, hydraulic transmission, cable systems, and line shafts remain important.

The present combined dwarven census is approximately 500,000–1,000,000. It is a historical population parameter, not an evolutionary effective population size or a calculated planetary carrying capacity. Viability requires distributed breeding networks, realistic age structure and reproductive success, regional industrial specialization, transport, food surpluses, and enough trained maintainers. No new catastrophe is inferred to explain the census; its demographic history remains to be developed.

## Hexagonal and honeycomb design principle

Honeycomb and sixfold forms are a selected recurring motif, used where function supports them. Brood-nursery respiratory panels may have rounded pores and locally near-hexagonal spacing, with irregular cellular ribs between thin mineral-organic skins. Rounded openings reduce sharp stress concentrations; short, connected gas paths matter more than geometric perfection. A curved casing requires defects and changes of cell shape, and fine protective meshes add diffusion resistance. Similar polygonal packing may appear regionally in tissues. Load-bearing bone remains an anisotropic, remodelable composite aligned to stress, not a regular hexagonal lattice.

Dwarven builders extend the same logic to cellular doors and floors, hatchery panels, ventilation grilles, filters, heat exchangers, furnace matrices, acoustic baffles, modular partitions, and arrays of circular shafts. District plans and retained-rock pillar webs may be honeycomb-like where geology permits. Major tunnels and caverns remain round, elliptical, arched, or geology-following because structural performance takes priority over the motif.

The brood-cell pattern becomes a cultural image of protected life, breath, kinship, and mutual support. It can recur in masonry, screens, textiles, civic emblems, and planning. It does not imply sixfold body symmetry, an insect-like caste system, a hive mind, compulsory groups of six, or identical architecture among all dwarven peoples.

## Native dwarven evolutionary body plan

### Settled canon

The dwarf-bearing clade is bilaterally symmetrical, ancestrally six-limbed, and supported by a mineralized internal skeleton. Its aquatic ancestors possess anterior, middle, and posterior pairs of appendages with distinct supporting girdles before terrestrialization. This is a native evolutionary lineage, not an Earth tetrapod that acquires an extra pair of arms. Three bilateral pairs do not constitute radial sixfold symmetry.

The adopted sequence is aquatic animal with three paired appendage fields, reinforced bottom-clinging fins, amphibious six-limbed shoreline animal, terrestrial six-limbed radiation, surface-fed cave-and-gorge climber, four-handed facultative biped, and habitually bipedal dwarven genus. The anterior pair becomes the upper precision-biased arms, the middle pair becomes the lower power-and-bracing arms, and the posterior pair becomes the principal weight-bearing legs.

All five native dwarf species and the deep-massif subspecies inherit four arms and two legs. They normally walk bipedally on suitable ground but use lower hands for climbing, bracing, hauling, and balance recovery. All six extremities can make contact during a sufficiently crouched scramble or across surrounding rock surfaces; this is not their routine walking stance. Both arm pairs retain manipulation, with upper precision bias and lower power bias rather than an absolute division of ability. Exact limb lengths, joint ranges, and girdle construction remain open.

The six-limbed ancestry does not assign six limbs to every animal phylum. Four-limbed animals can still exist through separate ancestry or secondary limb-pair reduction, with their relationships to be designed. A four-legged animal with a separate wing pair could now be a non-dwarven branch of the same six-limbed radiation; it no longer requires a separate phylum merely on limb-count grounds. Flight still requires its own aerodynamic and energetic assessment.

The conservative anatomical reconstruction uses a compact but deep, moderately elongated torso rather than a human chest with extra sockets. Upper and lower arm girdles are vertically staggered, with separate muscle attachments and braced load paths into the thoracic skeleton and spine. Upper arms principally work in the higher reach envelope; lower arms principally brace and manipulate near the trunk. Joint ranges overlap without requiring the arms to pass through one another. Relatively extended hips and knees, broad plantigrade feet, a reinforced pelvis, strong tendons, and short unsupported lever arms reduce sustained bending loads under 1.53 g. Lower palms have durable contact pads for occasional support; delicate fingertips need not carry body weight. These are working anatomical constraints, not a finished musculoskeletal model.

Four arms principally add simultaneous gripping, stabilization, and task sharing. They do not double strength at equal mass or improve unsupported balance automatically. A conservative respiratory model uses internally ventilated lungs, a dedicated ventilatory muscle system that is not locked by either arm girdle, and separated oxygenated and deoxygenated circulation; one central heart is sufficient in principle. A mammal-like tidal arrangement is an adequate starting analogue, while unidirectional airflow remains optional. For scaling only, a 60 kg adult weighs about 902 N here, equivalent to the static Earth weight of roughly 92 kg. Mature stature and mass remain open: broad joints, muscle allocation, gait, cardiac output, food, and heat rejection must be evaluated together.

### Open reproductive decision

A water-retaining enclosed embryo evolves before dry-upland radiation. The preferred provisional dwarven model is one yolk-provisioned egg incubated in a parent-built porous mineral nursery, followed by a small, highly dependent hatchling fed and protected by caregivers. The true egg membranes and shell control embryonic exchange; the external brood geode provides physical protection and a managed microclimate. Selected stone can become obligatory nesting material through evolved behavior and nursery architecture without requiring direct embryonic feeding from external rock. All six limbs develop and fit within the egg in flexed positions. Laying earlier reduces retained developmental time, not the size of a fully provisioned egg. Exact egg mass, incubation time, and post-hatching feeding anatomy remain open.

## Evolution of dwarf intelligence

### Settled canon

Dwarf ancestors were cave-centered rather than cave-isolated. Natural deep caves lack enough energy to support the evolution of large-brained, permanently subterranean populations without surface-linked feeding.

Important selection pressures include complex three-dimensional routes, patchy food and water, irregular floods, volcanic hazards, dangerous lake events, cooperative defense and storage, tool-assisted excavation, long juvenile learning, and cumulative social transmission.

Vision is retained. Dwarves combine low-light sight with strong hearing, vibration sensitivity, smell, artificial lighting, and continued surface or near-surface foraging.

Brain evolution does not require reliable fire from its outset. Tool-assisted food preparation, nutrient-dense aquatic and terrestrial foods, and simpler processing can increase returns before controlled combustion. Fermentation and geothermal cooking may later contribute where materials and temperatures permit, but are not guaranteed ancient technologies or necessary universal stages. Secure nutrition, parental provisioning, and social learning must precede dependence on complex food processing.

## Dwarven genus and living forms

### Settled canon

The desired taxonomy is five full species and six recognized living forms. The deep-massif form is a subspecies of the central-massif species.

The central-massif species is the nominal ecological generalist.

The deep-massif subspecies originated in surface-fed aquifers and caverns tens to hundreds of metres deep, not in kilometre-deep closed ecosystems. Candidate adaptations include flexible endogenous activity rhythms, greater sensory reliance on hearing and touch, and efficient energy use during inactivity. Modestly altered carbon-dioxide tolerance is possible but not established; it must not substitute for ventilation or impair acid-base balance. The form retains vision, surface-linked feeding ancestry, and full fertility with central-massif populations.

The sunward species is adapted to hot, arid margins through water conservation, efficient heat loss, sparse insulation, and somewhat longer extremities while remaining compact by Earth standards.

The volcanic-range species is the sunward species’ closest relative. It is the least compact and most gracile member of the genus in relative terms, retaining robust load-bearing anatomy. Plausible specializations include heat-shock and repair responses, flexible heat loss, salt and water regulation, protective mucus and cilia, tear protection, and element-specific uptake, binding, and excretion mechanisms. These can improve tolerance of particular chronic exposures, with biological costs; ancestry does not confer universal detoxification. Heat avoidance and cooled refuges remain essential, especially in humid conditions. Acute hydrogen sulfide, carbon monoxide, oxygen displacement, ash injury, pyroclastic flows, and major eruptions remain dangerous.

The nightward species evolved near the cold but still photic edge, not in isolated far-nightside oases. It has short distal limbs, strong insulation, non-shivering thermogenesis, heat-retaining vascular arrangements, and nasal heat exchange.

The high-mountain species evolved in naturally unpressurized cold highlands. At 2.5–3 km on this planet, pressure and oxygen availability can resemble roughly 4–5 km elevation on Earth because the atmospheric scale height is smaller under higher gravity. Credible adaptations include a deep chest, effective lung exchange, high capillary supply, hypoxia regulation that avoids pathological excess red-cell production, and improved embryonic oxygen delivery.

### Phylogeny and isolation

The central-massif species and its deep subspecies form one branch. A warm branch divides into sunward and volcanic species. A cold-upland branch divides into nightward and high-mountain species.

Species formation requires ancient allopatry or strong semigeographic isolation created by uplift, salt basins, arid barriers, glaciation, volcanism, and fragmented wet corridors, followed by secondary contact. Ecological gradients alone are insufficient for five durable species.

Occasional hybridization is a plausible working model. Central and deep-massif populations remain freely fertile. The five-species design requires persistent ecological and mating barriers across enough of each range; neither a divergence date nor geographic distance alone predicts hybrid fertility. Fertility may be sex-specific, asymmetric, or variable among populations. Recent rail links increase contact but need not immediately erase older population structure. The exact compatibility matrix remains unselected.

## Provisional evolutionary timescale

Selected chronological scaffold for a system approximately 7–9 billion years old; relative causal order is stronger than any assigned date. The intervals below describe the chosen fictional history and are neither an evolutionary clock nor estimates of the most probable timing of extraterrestrial life.

Microbial ecosystems and several phototrophic strategies are established more than 5 billion years before the present.

Sustained oxygenic photosynthesis develops roughly 4–5 billion years before the present.

Diverse aquatic macroscopic life is established roughly 3–4 billion years before the present.

The six-limbed shoreline clade and reproduction independent of standing external water arise roughly 2–3 billion years before the present in the existing provisional chronology. Embryos still require retained water and suitable exchange membranes. The three appendage pairs originate earlier in aquatic ancestors; no precise date is assigned to that developmental innovation.

Major terrestrial and cavern radiations unfold roughly 1–2 billion years before the present.

The endothermic, socially denning dwarf-parent clade appears roughly 80–200 million years before the present.

Four-handed, facultatively bipedal cave-and-gorge omnivores appear roughly 6–12 million years before the present. This is limb specialization within an already six-limbed lineage, not the origin of the extra arm pair.

Habitual bipedality and cumulative tool traditions develop roughly 3–4 million years before the present.

A recognizable dwarven genus appears roughly 2.5–3.5 million years before the present.

Living species diverge mainly within the last roughly 0.5–2 million years. The deep-massif subspecies differentiates within roughly the last 150–400 thousand years.

Long intervals permit repeated radiations, extinction, environmental change, and replacement of habitats. They do not imply billions of years of anatomical stasis, steadily increasing intelligence, or an inevitable dwarf endpoint. The geode specialization is much later than the first enclosed terrestrial eggs. Dates must be updated together if the stellar or geological history changes.

## Corrections and supersessions

The Thal catastrophe is not part of the planet’s geological or native evolutionary history.

The adopted six-limbed ancestry supersedes the earlier four-limbed dwarf-bearing-clade assumption. Geological history, planetary parameters, broad evolutionary time ranges, and the five-species/six-form radiation remain unchanged. Exact anatomy and resource budgets must be checked against the revised body plan.

The moons are not the main source of tectonics, mantle heat, or volcanism.

The 600 km nightward macroscopic margin is not continuously illuminated and is not a continuous photosynthetic biome.

Fungal cultivation converts organic feedstock; it is not an independent source of staple calories.

Chemosynthesis can dominate isolated local ecosystems but cannot power most of the planetary biosphere or deep dwarven civilization.

Near-Earth oxygen partial pressure does not guarantee Earth-like fire behavior; oxygen fraction, fuel, pressure, airflow, moisture, and temperature must be evaluated together.

Large underground cities at kilometre depths are engineered, surface-supported ecosystems rather than naturally self-sufficient caves.

The present population of 500,000–1,000,000 does not represent the ancient effective population required for natural speciation.

## Current scientific verdict

The integrated setting is a scientifically motivated speculative inhabited synchronously rotating super-Earth. Its major assumptions include the old quiet early-M host, retained moderate atmosphere and limited water, long-lived but not guaranteed mobile-lid tectonics, oxygenic photosynthesis under an M-dwarf spectrum, and a native six-limbed lineage that later produces compact, four-armed intelligent bipeds. The last is a selected worldbuilding hypothesis with biological analogues for its component mechanisms, not an observed or demonstrably probable extraterrestrial outcome.

The revised qualitative design has no identified necessary contradiction after the specified repairs, but remains a candidate rather than a jointly validated inhabited planet. One coherent stellar-orbital and present-atmosphere reference case now closes the independent-input arithmetic: the selected bulk planet derives 15.03 m/s² surface gravity, 16.37 km/s escape speed, a 9,979-kg/m² atmospheric column, and about 9.98 × 10^18 kg total atmosphere. Early volatile survival remains open because high escape speed does not solve pre-main-sequence hydrodynamic escape. The next model must reproduce the present water and atmosphere through stellar XUV history, magma and mantle exchange, oxidation, escape, impacts, and outgassing before climate and water return are tested. Native adult/brood physiology and Thal establishment at 1.53 g remain later gates.

## The cohesive surface geography

The useful organizing features are rock provinces, drainage divides and refuge connections, not Earth-style continents separated by global oceans. Substellar longitude is zero. Distance dayward from the geometric terminator determines the ideal direct-light elevation: altitude of the star above a level horizon is approximately distance divided by planetary radius, in radians. Actual terrain, clouds, scattering and atmospheric absorption modify received light.

Use these recurring landscapes, allowing multiple examples around the planet rather than assigning one biological species to each region:

Landscape: Hot central basin country; Physical construction: Broad basaltic plains, old shields, eroded uplands, interior salt basins, dunes and dry channels; Ecological and settlement role: Mostly barren exposed ground with microbial refuges; no continuous open-water sea or large-animal biome. Fresh volcanic terrain is local, not the whole hemisphere.

Landscape: Sunward terminal country; Physical construction: Rivers end in lower evaporative basins; alluvial fans, salt flats and limited persistent brines; Ecological and settlement role: Warm-margin foragers and specialist microbes occupy tolerable margins. Salt and heat create barriers between productive valleys.

Landscape: Illuminated massif-and-lake provinces; Physical construction: Competent old intrusive/metamorphic cores beside sedimentary catchments, tectonic lakes, aquifers and fertile foothills; Ecological and settlement role: Principal food production, dwarf cities and accessible Thal refuges. Discharge and light must be demonstrated for each inhabited valley.

Landscape: Cold illuminated uplands; Physical construction: Higher or more nightward lit slopes, sheltered valleys, snow-fed headwaters and glacial margins; Ecological and settlement role: Native cold/upland forms; isolated breeding refuges. Sunward-facing slopes can remain productive while adjacent shaded terrain is frozen.

Landscape: Active arc districts; Physical construction: Patchy volcanic chains, calderas, fractured aquifers, mineral soils and geothermal anomalies; Ecological and settlement role: Specialized volcanic dwarves on older cool flanks; mines and selected geothermal works. No obligatory co-location of homes and active vents.

Landscape: Dark marginal drainage country; Physical construction: Outlet-glacier corridors, cold aquifers, proglacial lakes and dry divides close to the terminator; Ecological and settlement role: Small detritus-fed communities and supplied settlements. Individual lakes can be ice-covered; darkness excludes local photosynthetic staple production.

Landscape: Deep-night ice basins and desert plateaus; Physical construction: A few thick ice accumulations separated by cold, wind-scoured or thinly frosted high ground; Ecological and settlement role: Sparse warm chemical oases and exceptional supplied outposts. Thick ice and basal water are regional, not universal.

Mountain chains may cross the illumination boundary obliquely; they should not form an unbroken wall exactly following the terminator. This permits valleys to connect lit catchments with cold stores and avoids assigning the same windward/leeward behavior everywhere. Divide crossings, river capture, glacier advances and salt-basin expansion provide credible historical isolation and reconnection. Present ridges and lakes are transient features within a long-lived climate regime, not unchanged habitats lasting billions of years.

Early natural cave habitats occur in carbonate layers, old lava tubes, fractures and shelters adjacent to productive waters. Later excavated cities extend into sound granite-like intrusions and gneiss. Habitable cave entrances, ore, strong construction rock and groundwater are related opportunities, not interchangeable properties of one magical rock type.

Adopt 240 million km³ water-equivalent as the reference bookkeeping case within the established 200–267-million-km³ range. This is a new test scenario, not a climate prediction. “Water-equivalent” avoids confusing ice volume with water mass. Bound mantle water is excluded. Accessible groundwater means the exchangeable upper-crustal reservoir, not that every part is cold, fresh or pumpable.

The lake example is eight large basins averaging 100,000 km² and 200 m depth, plus 1.2 million km² of smaller lakes averaging about 117 m: approximately 0.30 million km³ overall. Exact lake counts are illustrative; the established 50,000–150,000-km² size class is retained. About 2 million km² of open/lake area is only 0.2% of planetary area and 0.8% of the provisional corridor. It does not silently turn that corridor into a global ocean.

For scale, concentrating the nightside reservoir over 80 million km² gives about 3.0 km mean ice thickness at density 917 kg/m³. This is one way to make regional thick ice possible with a small total inventory. It is not proof that ice dynamics would maintain this footprint. A steady conductive comparison with ice conductivity 651/T W/(m·K), surface 220 K and geothermal flux 0.05 W/m² reaches an unpressurized 273-K base at roughly 2.8 km. Pressure, advected cold ice, bed conditions and heat transport alter that threshold. The comparison justifies testing some wet beds and leaving others frozen. [Menou’s ice model](https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.6472)

The reference geography therefore places accumulation basins and outlet valleys so that some glaciers can deliver water toward warmer margins. Freshwater reaches lit refuges by downhill meltwater and connected aquifers where hydraulic head allows it. Atmospheric precipitation is additional supply only where the circulation produces it. A glacier can also consume a valley or dam its river; its presence is not a guarantee of beneficial irrigation.

Storage closure is not flux closure. For each reservoir use change in storage = total inflow − total outflow. Track atmospheric deposition/ablation, ice transport, melt/refreezing, basin runoff, evaporation, groundwater exchange, escape and mantle exchange. An unbalanced transfer of 1,000 km³/year exhausts 240 million km³ in 240,000 years. A large reservoir cannot conceal an unbalanced billion-year hydrological story. Accept a climate/topography case only if some inhabited provinces persist or migrate through ordinary landscape turnover without complete cold trapping or runaway drying. [Water-trapping dependence on geography](https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.0540)

### Light and refuge location

The radius is 8,919.4 km. The 3,900/600-km corridor occupies about 24.5% of the spherical surface, approximately 245 million km². This is a geometric envelope, not its cultivated area. At 100–300 km dayward, ideal stellar elevation is only 0.64–1.93 degrees; long shadows and atmospheric path length make reliable agriculture difficult. Prefer initial inhabited-valley trials around 1,500–3,000 km dayward, where the ideal elevation is about 9.6–19.3 degrees, then use moderate elevation, aspect and shading to obtain cooler local conditions. These trial positions must move if the coupled climate fails.

Thal permanent mountain/cave settlement does not require extreme altitude. Begin with inhabited benches around 500–1,200 m above the local pressure datum, modest rock cover and access to higher seasonal/work sites. With the illustrative 280-K isothermal atmosphere, 1,000 m gives about 1.25 bar and 0.175–0.187 bar dry oxygen. This reduces hypoxic burden relative to a 3-km citadel; it does not remove high gravity or establish reproductive safety. No universal lapse rate is used to promise the desired temperatures.

## A regional economy that can be checked

A 25,000-person demonstration district connects one mountain-interior town to a larger watershed, farms and lakes. It is a capacity example, not a new census allocation or named city.

- A 20,000-km² catchment with 100 mm/year net runoff supplies 2 km³/year, or 63 m³/s annual mean. Net runoff is what remains after relevant losses, not rainfall assumed to fall on every slope. Low flows and reservoir operation must be supplied independently.

- Diverting 10 m³/s through 200 m head at 70% efficiency yields about 21 MW before further distribution losses. A high annual mean does not establish that this diversion is continuously available. Downstream users, fish passage, sediment and ecological flows require allowances.

- At an illustrative 3,500 kcal/person/day, food demand is 134 TJ/year, or 4.24 MW of ingested chemical power. A net edible yield of 1–5 MJ/m²/year implies 27–134 km² of effective productive capture area before reserves, non-food uses and unusable ground. This is a sensitivity range, not measured dwarf crop yield.

- Fungal edible output comes from a portion of the already-grown substrate. Its respiration and losses remain in the same accounting; no second full calorie harvest is added to the crop budget.

- Removing 25 MW of heat with a 5-K rise in cooling water needs approximately 1.2 m³/s. This flow must reach a real colder sink, with pumping and refrigeration work counted. Power consumed below ground ultimately contributes heat; warm mine water cannot be called an unlimited coolant.

- Ventilation, drainage, sanitation, ore-processing emissions, replacement equipment and skilled labor receive separate budgets. Combustion is isolated from residential and nursery air. Paired routes and distributed reserves prevent one intake, penstock, bridge or light collector from becoming the whole settlement’s lifeline.

The existing D7 local plan is a facility diagram, not the whole regional food-and-water catchment. Industrial links use mainline routes between clustered provinces rather than continuous settlement around an approximately 56,000-km ring. Remote dark outposts remain few, small and expensive to supply. A million people can operate concentrated industrial systems; that population does not imply they can maintain an Earth-scale continental infrastructure network.

## Biology and the Thals: close the causal gaps

Retain the six-limbed ancestry, staggered arm girdles, braced trunk, broad feet, dedicated ventilation and one central heart as the minimum coherent anatomical reconstruction. The evolutionary explanation needs a foraging benefit for freeing the second manipulative pair, not just an appeal to later tool use. Three-dimensional climbing, stabilizing food near the trunk and simultaneous handling provide a testable hypothesis. No full musculoskeletal model, embryo or physiological simulation has been produced.

The favored nursery has an actual egg, an air space and a parent-built mineral-organic casing with protected short gas paths. It develops from simpler functional nests before sapient construction. Wetting a porous shell changes gas transport drastically; the external casing cannot compensate for an inadequate true eggshell. Adults supply organic calories after hatching. Calcareous shell material may supply some skeletal mineral by analogy, while external grit is optional controlled nutrition later. Exact egg mass, incubation and hatchling mass stay open until total conductance, water loss, waste storage and heat all agree. No special gem mineral is required to create life. [Comparative eggshell conductance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4035113/)

The Thals’ arrival scenario now includes a finite establishment package of compatible Erde seeds/propagules, food cultures, associated cultivation organisms and initial provisions, followed by local cultivation. This is a proposed historical bridge that preserves the migration and independent native evolution without assuming that alien vegetation is a balanced human diet. Dwarven hosts can provide shelters, water and labor without automatically supplying compatible staple calories. No continuing portal-based food subsidy is necessary once cultivation works.

Protect cultivated substrate and irrigation from unsuitable salts, metals and native pests. Select crops for spectrum and temperature; use shutters or covered rooms to restore dark periods where required. Manage crop residues within the compatible system. Native foods can enter Thal diets only after their digestibility, essential nutrients and toxins are understood. The amount and diversity of transferred material, founding population, generation structure, early harvest failures and reserves remain explicit historical design tasks. [Evidence that continuous-light response differs among crop genotypes](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms5549)

Do not solve Thal physiology by claiming complete genetic transformation in five millennia. Cultural accommodation can be immediate; selection depends on heritable variation and surviving reproduction. Lower mountain benches, assisted freight, water access, rest and maternity support reduce demands. Animal centrifugation results neither prove human impossibility nor demonstrate healthy multigenerational life at 1.53 g. This remains one of the strongest unsupported premises in the otherwise naturalistic inhabited setting. [Rat reproduction studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23350080/)

Languages, nursery symbolism, underground preference and species relationships are preserved. Hamarkor/Hamarkorar is selected; Hamarkorvar and Korvar retain their meanings. Rakkor and Menkor remain geographically unassigned. No plate, river basin, species or gene forces a particular language, polity or moral character.
