# The Hamarkorar

## People of the great rock masses

**Hamarkor** means a person of the crags or great rock masses. **Hamarkorar** is the plural and the general name for the native dwarven peoples of Dverghamar. It includes farmers, musicians, travelers and people living in many kinds of terrain.

**Rakkor/Rakkorar** is a regional people-name whose community has not yet been specified. **Menkor/Menkorar** is retained for a narrower local, cultural or species identity, also awaiting a specific assignment. **Hamkor** remains a proposed conversational shortening of Hamarkor.

## Four arms and two legs

The Hamarkorar descend from six-limbed animals. Their hind limbs carry most of the weight during upright movement; the other two pairs have become arms. Staggered limb girdles, a supportive trunk and breathing muscles that work independently of the arms are the proposed mechanical basis for this speculative body plan; its full physiology remains unvalidated.

The upper pair is especially suited to precise manipulation and the lower pair to bracing and carrying, while both retain dexterity. Four arms allow a person to hold, steady and work on something at the same time. Their strength still depends on muscle, leverage, body mass and the demands of the planet’s gravity.

Exact adult dimensions, finger counts and external coverings have not yet been specified. The established anatomy provides a foundation for those details without making the Hamarkorar human bodies with extra arms attached.

## Related peoples in different habitats

The native genus contains five species and a deep-massif subspecies.

| Population | Principal environmental pressures |
| --- | --- |
| Central massif species | Varied mountain interiors, slopes and connected surface catchments |
| Deep-massif subspecies | Greater reliance on enclosed habitats, while retaining connections to shallower ecosystems |
| Sunward species | Dry heat, water conservation and warm-margin travel |
| Volcanic-range species | Humid heat, ash and locally distinctive chemical exposure |
| Cold-edge species | Cold-margin conditions near the productive twilight belt |
| High-mountain species | Altitude, reduced oxygen availability and cold |

Their ranges can overlap. Languages, communities and political affiliations do not follow species boundaries automatically. The deep-massif population remains a subspecies of the central massif species, with fertile exchange where populations meet.

## Adaptation and senses

Sunward dwarves conserve water and shed heat with relatively sparse insulation and somewhat longer extremities, while remaining compact by Earth standards. Volcanic-range dwarves are the genus’s most slender form in relative terms. Protective mucus, cilia, tear protection and substance-specific uptake or excretion offer possible defenses against chronic exposure. Heat avoidance and cooled refuges remain essential; acute gases, ash injury and eruptions remain dangerous.

Cold-edge dwarves have short extremities, strong insulation, heat-retaining circulation, nasal heat exchange and non-shivering heat production. High-mountain dwarves face reduced oxygen as well as cold. Their physiological reconstruction favors effective lung exchange, capillary supply and hypoxia regulation, including oxygen delivery to developing young.

Vision is retained across the genus. Hearing, vibration sensitivity, smell, touch and artificial lighting complement low-light sight. The deep-massif subspecies originated in surface-fed aquifers and caverns tens to hundreds of meters deep. Flexible activity rhythms and economical inactive periods are candidate adaptations; enhanced carbon-dioxide tolerance has not been established.

## The mineral nursery

Stone has a place in the preferred reconstruction of dwarven reproduction. Parents build a porous mineral nursery around a **true biological egg**. The egg’s membrane and shell, the constructed casing and the nursery as a whole serve different purposes.

Connected pores allow oxygen to enter and carbon dioxide to leave. Honeycombed supports can strengthen a casing without filling every space with solid material. Caregivers must also maintain moisture and temperature, remove waste and allow the hatchling a safe exit.

The embryo is nourished by organic reserves; after hatching, the dependent young require organic food. Long adaptation and accumulated material knowledge help dwarves select suitable local minerals. Their tolerances need not match human tolerances, but remain specific to particular substances and conditions.

The nursery is a promising biological model rather than a fully quantified life cycle. Egg size, incubation duration, shell chemistry, feeding anatomy and the balance between parental work and biological construction remain to be described.

## Household life and material knowledge

Dwelling within rock encourages close attention to cracks, grain, moisture, heat and the behavior of a material under load. This knowledge appears in language, construction and nursery care. Hexagonal arrangements suit some supports, compartments and markings, while other shapes serve tunnels, water channels and rooms better.

Kinship and care extend beyond a single prescribed household form. Korvar distinguishes reproductive parentage, protective care, food provision and shared residence. A person can hold several of these roles.

## Nursery air and provisioning

A ventilated air gap separates the egg’s physiological shell from the load-bearing outer casing. Short connected gas-filled pores must remain open and sufficiently dry; a thick wet mineral wall is not automatically breathable. The egg carries organic reserves, and the hatchling leaves it small and dependent on feeding. Material preparation supports reproduction but does not replace biological development. Peak oxygen demand, water loss, waste storage and safe hatching remain separate constraints.
