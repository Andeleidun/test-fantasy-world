# Dverghamar: climate, geology and settlement

## The star and the planet

Dverghamar’s physical setting is a rocky super-Earth around an old early-M star. The star is smaller, cooler and dimmer than the Sun. Its relative quiet describes its mature state, not its youth: a viable planetary history still requires enough atmosphere and water to survive earlier stellar activity.

| Quantity | Working value |
| --- | --- |
| Planetary mass | About 3 Earth masses |
| Planetary radius | About 1.4 Earth radii |
| Surface gravity | About 1.53 Earth gravities |
| Stellar mass | About 0.4–0.6 solar masses |
| System age | About 7–9 billion years |
| Rotation | Synchronous, with a provisional orbital period of 30–60 Earth days |
| Surface pressure | About 1.5 bar |
| Oxygen fraction | About 14–15% of the dry atmosphere |
| Exchangeable surface and near-surface water | About 0.15–0.20 Earth oceans |

These values describe a scenario to be tested together. Orbital period, stellar luminosity, atmospheric circulation and received heat cannot be chosen independently. The oxygen fraction gives a dry partial pressure of roughly 0.21–0.225 bar at the reference surface pressure. Higher gravity means that 1.5 bar does not imply an atmospheric column one and a half times Earth’s mass per unit area.

The water estimate includes lakes, rivers, accessible groundwater, glaciers and nightside ice. Water chemically bound in the deep mantle is a separate, unresolved reservoir.

Satellite and companion configurations remain unspecified. Their orbital stability and influence on eccentricity need to be evaluated with the eventual stellar and planetary parameters.

## Rock, relief and geological time

Basaltic crust, volcanic arcs, intrusive granite-like bodies, gneiss-rich massif cores and tectonic lake basins provide the principal geological settings. Uplift and erosion expose different rocks and produce the relief that directs rainfall, drainage and movement.

Long-lived geological cycling helps replenish weathered materials and sustain chemical energy sources. The exact tectonic regime remains an open part of the physical reconstruction; a rocky super-Earth does not automatically have Earth-style plate tectonics.

Mountain relief of roughly 1–3 km is common in the settlement model, with major ranges reaching 3–4.5 km and rare exceptional relief of 5–6 km. These are local height differences, not a completed global elevation map. Higher gravity makes rock strength, faulting and erosion particularly consequential.

The biosphere develops under synchronous illumination from near its beginning. Low obliquity and small eccentricity sustain long-lived day and night hemispheres, with small librations and orbital weather variations possible near their boundary. Dverghamar’s history does not require a recent catastrophe that abruptly established permanent day and night. The native biosphere develops within a long-lived planetary environment. The much later Thal catastrophe occurred on Erde.

## Heat, light and the twilight corridor

| Environment | Working surface range |
| --- | --- |
| Central dayside lowlands | 340–375 K, or about 67–102°C |
| Exceptional dark dayside basins | Approaching 385 K, or about 112°C |
| Deep nightside | 205–230 K, or about −68 to −43°C |
| Local nightside cold traps | 190–205 K, or about −83 to −68°C |

The proposed corridor for exposed large life is about **4,500 km wide**, provisionally extending **3,900 km sunward** and **600 km nightward** from the geometric terminator. This is a target envelope, not a climate result or a continuous strip of fertile land. Local relief, water availability and atmospheric circulation can shift its useful boundaries substantially.

The asymmetry reflects light as well as temperature. Sunward terrain retains an energy source for photosynthesis over a broad range of conditions. Beyond the terminator, tolerable temperatures alone do not sustain a large food web. Most production remains within the lit wet core, while the darker fringe relies on transported food and connected habitats.

Cloud and atmospheric heat transport must allow this contrast without freezing all accessible water into permanent nightside storage or making the dayside wet enough to erase the intended dry heat. Those coupled balances are still unvalidated.

## Rivers, lakes and salt country

Mountain catchments feed rivers, aquifers and lakes. Several large ecological provinces occupy wetter ground, connected by narrower valleys and passes. Mountain rain shadows open into closed evaporative basins, producing the long-lived arid barriers between them.

Permanent terminal brine lakes occur only on the outer dayside. Farther inward, river-end basins become episodic salt pans. The largest surviving freshwater or less saline natural lakes cover about **50,000–150,000 km²** each; many smaller lakes and wetlands accompany them.

Deep lakes commonly have oxygenated surface and coastal waters above permanently stratified, often anoxic depths. Partial mixing may occur over decades or centuries. Dangerous whole-lake overturns are exceptional events separated by millennia and associated with major disturbances. These timescales are setting targets; individual basins will depend on their depth, dissolved salts, circulation and heat budget.

Nightside ice stores water. Slow return through basal melting, groundwater and drainage must ultimately help prevent irreversible loss of the twilight water supply. The quantities and pathways remain an important part of the future water-cycle model.

## Producers and cultivated food

Photosynthetic organisms supply most usable energy to the large biosphere. They occupy lit slopes, shallow water, moist ground and other favorable parts of the twilight region. Their exact pigments, colors and leaf-like structures remain open; a redder star alone does not determine a single plant color.

Chemically powered microbes add production where geological reductants and oxidants meet. Fungi and fungal analogues recycle organic material and make useful crops, but their food must come from another producer or an imported organic source.

Storage-root or tuber crops suit protected cultivation when their photosynthetic tissues receive enough light. Light wells and near-surface chambers can shelter the growing medium while exposing leaves. Crop yield depends on the illuminated collecting area and useful light, not the amount of underground floor space. Mirrors and glazing incur losses; routine electric grow lights are too costly to supply staple calories at this technological level. Dark fungal chambers need feedstock, ventilation, moisture control and waste handling. Crop and fungal calories cannot be counted twice: part of the feedstock is consumed in respiration or remains as waste. Agricultural area and productivity still have to meet the population’s energy needs.

## Animals and the limits of the food web

Large lake animals commonly reach 2–5 meters and hundreds of kilograms, with rare forms approaching 1–3 tonnes. Buoyancy reduces the structural burden of high gravity, but population size still depends on production, oxygen and accessible feeding habitat. The anoxic depths are not a second ordinary fish community.

Cool aquifers allow natural macroscopic life to extend into the sunward margin. Deep dayside habitats have microbes only in the current reconstruction. Airborne microbes over both hostile hemispheres are mostly dormant travelers, with brief activity possible in rare liquid cloud droplets.

The deep nightside has rare active microbial sites near brine veins and geothermal fractures. Isolated geothermal oases obtain usable energy from local geological reductants plus slow oxidant delivery through ice and groundwater. The largest common consumers remain finger-length or smaller, sparse but active between melt events.

Slow basal melting can fill a reservoir until pressure breaks an ice dam. The resulting irregular flood may temporarily connect neighboring oases. A typical large connection occurs only every few centuries to several millennia, with smaller internal drainage events more frequent. Favorable deliveries of food and chemicals support temporary population blooms. Other floods can scour habitat, dilute useful chemicals or deplete oxygen. Poorer oases may contain microbes alone.

## Settlement inside the rock

The Hamarkorar prefer underground options when they can provide comparable living conditions. Surface facilities remain where light, cooling, river access or transport gives a clear practical advantage.

Ordinary residential cover is roughly **200–800 m**. Depth means thickness of overlying rock; elevation means height above a stated datum. A chamber high inside a mountain can therefore be easier to ventilate and cool than a much lower but deeply buried mine.

Deeper residential districts and rare strongholds reach roughly **1.5–2 km**, with **2.2–2.3 km** an exceptional engineering target. Mines may reach about **2.5 km** and rare expeditions roughly **3 km**; neither is equivalent to permanent residence. Rail usually follows shallower cover of about **30–200 m**.

Greater depth raises the costs of removing heat, moving air and water, supporting openings and evacuating people. Robust bodies improve some tasks but do not replace cooling machinery. The most ambitious works belong to an industrial capability broadly comparable to late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century engineering, with local differences in power use and organization.

Supplied outposts may extend thousands of kilometers nightward, with the longest provisional reaches around **3,000–4,000 km from the terminator**. This is an infrastructure-dependent reach, not an equally broad natural habitat or a self-sufficient agricultural zone.

## Power and supporting landscapes

Twilight mountain rivers provide most industrial power. Direct shafts, gears, belts, hydraulic systems and compressed air transmit useful work. Fuel engines operate especially where a surface installation can handle intake, exhaust and heat. Geothermal plants supplement suitable locations.

Limited electricity serves selected rail, communications, pumps, lighting and controls. This economy does not require ubiquitous electrical distribution or digital computing. Reliable ventilation, drainage, transport and lighting remain necessary whichever technology drives them.

A city’s supporting landscape includes farms, water intakes, power works, ventilation openings, waste treatment and transport routes. Its footprint is larger than its visible entrances. [The settlement maps](https://andeleidun.github.io/test-fantasy-world/dverghamar-atlas.html#d5) show how these relationships fit together.

## What remains to be measured

The strongest remaining physical questions concern the coupled climate and water cycle, early atmospheric survival, local geothermal gradients, food-web productivity and the full nursery life cycle. The adopted population range of roughly **500,000–1,000,000 native dwarves** is a census target, not the outcome of a carrying-capacity calculation.
